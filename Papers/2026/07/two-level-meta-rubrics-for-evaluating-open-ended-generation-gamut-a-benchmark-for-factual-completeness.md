# Two-Level Meta-Rubrics for Evaluating Open-Ended Generation: GAMUT, a Benchmark for Factual Completeness

## TL;DR
GAMUT is a benchmark for measuring factual completeness (recall) in long-form open-ended generation, using a novel two-level meta-rubric framework: structured, expressive rubrics are authored/verified by humans, then mechanically compiled into flat binary checks an LLM judge grades reliably. It contains 1,813 image-grounded 'everyday deep research' questions across 10 domains; even the best model (Gemini 3.1 Pro) reaches only 58.7%, showing factual recall is far from solved and largely invisible to precision-based evaluation.

## Abstract
> Evaluating the factuality of long-form generations has focused predominantly on precision, measuring whether the claims a model makes are correct. The dominant decompose-search-verify pipeline catches incorrect claims well but says little about whether a response contains all the information it should. Measuring factual completeness, the missing half of factuality, is harder: it requires enumerating the full set of facts a complete answer should contain, and these facts rarely form a flat list. They often involve open-ended sets where coverage is what matters, ordered processes, and relationships among facts that a list of independent boolean checks fails to capture. We introduce a two-level meta-rubric framework for evaluating open-ended generation, and instantiate it as Gamut (Grounded Assessment of Multimodal Factuality), a benchmark for factual completeness in long-form generation. The framework rests on a two-level rubric representation: a structured meta-rubric captures the organization and importance of the required content, which is then mechanically compiled into a flat checklist of binary, machine-gradable rubrics that an LLM judge scores reliably. We construct 1,813 questions grounded in real wearable imagery across 10 diverse domains, each paired with an evidence-backed rubric verified by expert human annotators. Because the framework is modality-agnostic, we also release a text-only variant. Evaluating 14 frontier and open-weight models, we find the benchmark genuinely challenging (best score 58.7% from Gemini 3.1 Pro), highly discriminative, and robust to the choice of judge.

## Highlights
- Best model (Gemini 3.1 Pro) reaches only 58.7% GAMUT score, meeting at most 55% of rubric elements — substantial headroom remains.
- The dominant failure mode is omission, not error: 'missing' verdicts account for ~2/3 of rubric elements for weak models and >1/4 even for the strongest; contradiction rates stay low (~3-4%) for top models but climb to 8-11% for weaker/open models.
- 98% of questions require at least one structured component beyond isolated facts (86% flexible lists, 49% strict lists, 17% processes, 6% relationships), proving a flat checklist would misrepresent most questions.
- Rubrics average 15.2 binary checks per question (5.9 Answer-Critical, 5.8 Valuable, 3.5 Context), grounded in ~10 web snippets each, citing 9,400+ distinct web pages total.
- Judge-robust: Gemini 3.1 Pro and Claude Opus 4.8 give identical rankings within 1.8 points and neither favors its own answers; Qwen3-VL 235B is uniformly ~4-11 points more lenient but preserves ordering.
- Text-only variant raises scores by a roughly constant 10-24 points across all models, showing visual identification is a 'constant tax' and the multimodal ranking reflects knowledge completeness, not perception.
- Wide capability spread: proprietary models (Gemini 3.1 Pro 58.7, GPT-5.4 42.6, GPT-4o 34.2) far outscore open-weight (Qwen3-VL 235B 33.0, Llama 4 Maverick 18.3, Llama 3.2 11B 5.1).

## Method
The core innovation is a two-level rubric representation. Level 1 is a structured meta-rubric describing what a complete answer needs, using five typed components: Simple Knowledge (single fact), Strict List (all items required), Flexible List (coverage of an open pool with a baseline threshold like 'at least N of...'), Process (ordered steps, mandatory + optional), and Relationship (connection between entities), plus optional 'meta-insights' (syntheses no single item captures). Each item carries an importance tier: Answer-Critical, Valuable, or Context, and cites human-verified web snippets. Level 2 mechanically compiles this into a flat list of self-contained binary pass/fail checks by deterministic rules: strict lists become one check per item; flexible lists become baseline threshold checks (fully enumerated in-text) plus per-item bonus checks for short pools (<=7) or higher coverage thresholds for long pools; processes produce per-step presence checks plus sequence/order checks; secondary items (optional steps, bonus coverage, full ordering) drop one importance tier. Scoring: an LLM judge returns one of four verdicts per check (meets=1, partially_meets=lambda=0.5, missing=0, contradicts=mu=-2), with contradictions penalized more than omissions so a cautious answer beats a confidently wrong one. Per-tier scores are combined with global weights (wAC=0.6, wV=0.3, wC=0.1), yielding a GAMUT score in [-1,1]. Dataset construction pairs Gemini 3.1 Pro (which does web search) with expert human annotators over multiple rounds. Questions are generated from 1,938 real CRAG-MM wearable/egocentric images across 10 domains, referring to entities only via pronouns/generic terms ('this car'), filtered by a 'stranger test' to reject leading questions, and diversified against boilerplate templates. Rubrics are LLM-generated with self-refinement (an audit phase re-verifies every snippet by browsing, an independent search phase re-researches from scratch), then human-revised (annotators edit the binary rubric directly while trained to reason in the structured space). Final: 1,813 questions with complete human-verified rubrics.

## Evals & Results
14 frontier and open-weight models evaluated on 1,813 multimodal questions, judged primarily by Gemini 3.1 Pro. Metrics: headline GAMUT score, per-tier scores, and verdict distributions. There are no external baselines since it is a new benchmark, but the paper positions itself against precision-focused DSV pipelines (FActScore, VeriScore) and rubric benchmarks like FACTS Multimodal (which uses only a flat essential/non-essential split). Key movements: rankings recover known capability regularities (newer/larger > older/smaller, proprietary > open-weight), the metric is highly discriminative with large margins, and rankings hold across three different judges (Gemini, Claude, Qwen). Qwen3-VL variants show highest contradiction rates; Claude Opus shows the lowest (~3%). The text-only variant (1,806 questions) confirms perception is a near-constant offset rather than the discriminating axis.

## So What (for practitioners)
When evaluating long-form or knowledge-seeking outputs, precision-only fact-checking misses the dominant failure mode — omission. Adopt a completeness-oriented rubric. The two-level design is a transferable recipe: author rich, structured criteria (open sets with coverage thresholds, ordered processes, importance tiers) but compile them into flat, self-contained binary checks before handing them to an LLM judge — this preserves expressiveness while capturing the low variance and high agreement of binary LLM-as-judge grading. Concrete engineering patterns worth reusing: separate 'missing' from 'contradicts' verdicts and penalize contradictions more heavily to reward calibrated caution; use global importance weights rather than per-question check counts so trivial context checks can't dominate; ground every rubric item in cited, human-verified evidence rather than parametric memory; and use an audit-then-independent-re-search refinement loop to catch incomplete lists. For RAG/agent systems, this benchmark reveals that models supply only ~55% of what a complete answer needs even at the frontier, so completeness (not just correctness) should be an explicit optimization and eval target. The full prompts (question generation, selection with a 'stranger test', rubric generation/refinement, judge, text-only conversion) are provided and directly adaptable.

## Open Questions / Critiques
The entire pipeline depends heavily on a single frontier model (Gemini 3.1 Pro) to generate questions, rubrics, and serve as the primary judge — despite human verification, this risks systematic blind spots or bias toward that model family (though the paper shows Gemini doesn't inflate its own scores). The 'ground truth' of what a complete answer requires is inherently subjective and defined by whatever evidence annotators gathered; open-ended flexible-list thresholds (e.g., 'at least 2 of N') and importance-tier assignments involve judgment calls that may not generalize. The scoring hyperparameters (lambda=0.5, mu=-2, tier weights 0.6/0.3/0.1) are chosen heuristically without ablation on human-alignment. Judge leniency varies substantially (Qwen ~4-11 points higher), so absolute scores are judge-dependent even if rankings are stable. Rubrics rest on web snippets that can go stale or be blocked (some cited pages already flagged 'Blocked'/'Invalid'), raising maintenance concerns. Finally, the benchmark measures completeness against a fixed reference set, so it may penalize valid facts outside the annotators' pool and cannot fully capture answer coherence or synthesis quality beyond the meta-insight checks.
